Theoretical Frameworks: Foucault, Baudrillard, and Beyond

Foucault: Episteme, Discipline, and Governmentality

Michel Foucault’s work offers two key insights for analyzing education historically: epistemes – the deep frameworks of knowledge that define each historical age – and disciplinary power – the techniques by which modern institutions regulate individuals. In The Order of Things (1966), Foucault identifies distinct epistemes that governed what could be known and said in different eras. For example, the Renaissance episteme was “characterized by resemblance and similitude” ￼; knowledge was based on interpreting an interconnected world of analogies (e.g. learning via symbols, allegory, divine correspondences). The Classical episteme of the 17th–18th centuries introduced a new emphasis on representation, classification, order and difference – think of the Enlightenment drive to catalog nature and human society in taxonomies ￼. Finally, the Modern episteme (19th century onward) emerged with the “invention of Man” as an object of knowledge ￼ – meaning that human beings became the focus of the sciences (biology, economics, linguistics, psychology, etc.), grounded in new notions of empirical observation and philosophical critique (after Kant). These epistemic shifts provide a backdrop for how education was conceived in each period: what counted as valid knowledge to teach, who was seen as a subject capable of learning, and what the purpose of education was (e.g. reflection of divine order, formation of rational citizen, etc.).

In addition to the historical epistemes, Foucault’s Discipline and Punish (1975) is seminal for understanding modern schooling as a disciplinary institution. Foucault showed that from the 18th–19th centuries, systems like prisons, factories, militaries – and indeed schools – converged on techniques of surveillance, normalization, and examination to produce “docile bodies”. Schools adopted regimented schedules, spatial partitioning (rows of desks, classrooms by age group), and constant supervision by teachers, analogous to soldiers in barracks or inmates in a Panopticon. The examination in particular combined hierarchical observation with normalizing judgment, making each student into a “case” with a score or grade – a prime example of knowledge as a means of power. Foucault argued that such techniques were not coincidental but part of a broader project of disciplining society, aligning with the needs of industrial capitalism and the nation-state to shape compliant, productive citizens.

A 19th-century diagram of Bentham’s Panopticon prison, which Foucault adopted as a metaphor for modern institutions. In schools, as in prisons, a central observer (teacher/authority) can potentially monitor all subjects, inducing a state of conscious and permanent visibility that assures the automatic functioning of power. Classrooms were often designed so that teachers’ gaze and examinations kept pupils under a form of panoptic surveillance, training them to self-regulate their behavior. ￼ ￼

Later, Foucault developed the concept of governmentality – the art of governing populations – which included education as a key apparatus. From the 18th century on, states increasingly saw schooling as a means to instill certain norms and manage the populace. For instance, the expansion of compulsory education in the 19th century can be viewed as a governmentality project: producing citizens with particular skills, values, and “moral orthopedics” (to use Foucault’s term for the quotidian ethical training in schools ￼). Education systems helped establish what Foucault called a “general politics” of truth in modern societies – privileging scientific discourse and credentialed experts (teachers, professors) as authorities of truth ￼ ￼. As Foucault wrote, “each society has its regime of truth” – the accepted methods and institutions by which truth is produced and circulated ￼. Schools and universities became central “apparatuses of education” through which true statements (curricula, textbooks) are disseminated and through which individuals are categorized by truth (e.g. graded as “pass” or “fail,” diagnosed as “gifted” or “remedial”). Thus, in Foucauldian terms, modern education operates at the nexus of power and knowledge, training individuals’ bodies and minds while also establishing the truths that define normalcy and success.

In summary, Foucault gives us two analytic axes for education’s history: one epistemic (how the very conception of knowledge/learning changes across epochs) and one disciplinary (how power techniques in education change, especially with the rise of mass schooling). We will use both axes to interpret the historical transformation of education and later to assess contemporary shifts (such as data-driven personalization) as possibly heralding a new episteme and new mode of power.

Baudrillard: Simulacra, Simulation, and Hyperreality

Where Foucault offers a history of knowledge and power, Jean Baudrillard offers a provocative history of images, signs, and reality. In Simulacra and Simulation (1981), Baudrillard delineates four stages of simulacra – a progression in how representations relate (or cease to relate) to reality ￼ ￼. This framework will help us understand education as a process that increasingly deals not with direct “truth” or utility, but with simulations and symbols (grades, credentials, digital data) that may lose grounding in real learning or ability.

Baudrillard’s four stages can be summarized as follows:
	1.	First-order simulacrum: the faithful copy. In this stage, a sign or image is believed to be a direct reflection of a profound reality ￼. Baudrillard calls this the “sacramental order” – the representation is sacred because it transparently points to something real (as a religious icon is meant to point to an actual saint or virtue). In pre-modern contexts (roughly the pre-Industrial era), education could be seen this way: knowledge taught was assumed to reflect truth about God or nature, and a scholar’s credentials (like a medieval degree) truly signified their learning. The purpose of education for elites was often to grasp an underlying order (e.g. the harmony of the cosmos, the truth of scripture), so there was a naïve unity between the sign (the lesson, the text, the certificate) and the reality (wisdom or skill).
	2.	Second-order simulacrum: the distorted or perverted copy. Here the sign is recognized as an unfaithful copy that masks or distorts reality ￼. In Baudrillard’s words, it “masks and denatures a profound reality” – an “evil appearance” ￼. This roughly corresponds to the modern era of mass production (19th to mid-20th century), where representations proliferate and can be manipulated. In education, we can think of how schooling in the industrial age often claimed to reflect merit and knowledge, but in fact served other purposes. For example, schools purported to be meritocratic (you get the grades you earn), yet as Bowles and Gintis argued, they largely reproduced social class relations behind the facade of merit ￼ ￼. The curriculum might extol enlightenment and equality while masking a hidden curriculum of obedience and stratification. A high school diploma in 1900 certainly meant you had certain skills, but it also became a generalized symbol – one could “pass” school without truly understanding the material, by conforming and regurgitating. In Baudrillard’s second stage, signs still refer to an underlying reality, but imperfectly; likewise, educational credentials in this period often denatured reality, hinting that a graduate was educated while hiding unequal access and rote learning techniques.
	3.	Third-order simulacrum: masking the absence of reality. At this stage, the sign pretends to be a faithful copy but has no original referent – it stands in for something that doesn’t actually exist ￼. Baudrillard calls this the “order of sorcery,” a regime of semantic algebra where signs endlessly refer to other signs ￼. This is the era of hyperreality, typically associated with late 20th-century postmodernity: think of how in the consumer society, brand images and media narratives create a world that feels real to us but may have no grounding (e.g. a TV reality show simulates family life, but it’s all staged; money becomes detached from gold and is just digits). In education, the third order simulacrum is vividly seen in phenomena like teaching to the test, grade inflation, and credentialism. A student’s test score or GPA becomes a simulated proxy for their knowledge – but it might “mask the absence of profound reality” ￼, i.e. the student with high grades may have learned only how to take tests, not deep understanding. A college degree becomes a requirement for jobs regardless of actual skills, so the degree itself is a sign that one is “competent” even if the job training happens entirely anew. In short, education in late modernity drifted into hyperreality: the rituals of schooling (standardized tests, GPAs, rankings) created a self-contained truth (your “academic profile”) which increasingly stands in for any practical reality. The map has overtaken the territory – as Baudrillard would say, the educational system produces maps of competence and intelligence (transcripts, scores) that precede and determine life outcomes, even if the “territory” (real-world ability) is absent or different ￼.
	4.	Fourth-order simulacrum: pure simulation with no relation to reality. In this final stage, signs no longer even pretend to relate to a reality; they only refer to one another in a “regime of total equivalency” ￼. Baudrillard describes this as a state where cultural products need not be real at all, because people’s lives are so saturated with artificiality that even claims to reality are phrased in hyperreal terms ￼. We can argue that 21st-century, AI-driven education is approaching this fourth order simulacrum. For example, in some fully online courses or diploma mills, a “student” might pay fees and complete automated modules to obtain a credential without any human interaction or verification – a closed loop of simulation where learning is just clicking through a program. Adaptive learning software can create a simulated personal tutor experience (a “robo-tutor in the cloud” as Knewton’s CEO quipped ￼), giving students the feeling of learning, generating copious data and feedback, even if the content has become secondary to the experience of the platform. A striking example is the emergence of purely algorithmic assessment: if an AI grading system scores an essay, students quickly learn to write for the algorithm, producing an essay that gets a high score even if it is gibberish to a human – a perfect simulacrum of a good essay with no real substance. In the fourth order, education becomes a simulation game: points, badges, dashboards, and recommendation algorithms create a self-contained reality of “personalized learning” that may not connect to any external benchmark. As we will discuss, Baudrillard’s notion of the hyperreal rings true in the age of AI tutors and infinite test prep – the process is real to the participants, but the referent (genuine mastery, enlightenment) can evaporate.

Baudrillard’s simulacra thus provide a way to periodize the evolution of education’s meaning. From a time when schooling was a sincere transmission of truth (first order), through an era of mass schooling with ideological distortions (second order), into the late 20th century’s emphasis on performative metrics (third order), and now into an era where the form of education (the system of personalized, data-driven feedback) might stand on its own as a self-justifying simulation (fourth order). We will leverage these distinctions to interpret concrete changes in schooling practices.

Bowles & Gintis: Schooling as Social Reproduction

Any analysis of mass education must account for its socio-economic function. Samuel Bowles and Herbert Gintis, in Schooling in Capitalist America (1976), offered a Marxian critique that complements Foucault’s focus on discipline. They argued that the primary role of education in capitalist societies is the reproduction of the labor force and social hierarchy, not the egalitarian ideal of meritocracy. This is encapsulated in their “correspondence theory”: the structure of schooling mirrors the workplace to prepare students for their future exploitation ￼ ￼. For instance, schools have hierarchies (administrators over teachers, teachers over students) that correspond to bosses and workers ￼. Students learn to work for external rewards (grades) rather than intrinsic satisfaction, just as workers labor for a wage ￼. They have little control over curriculum content, analogous to workers’ lack of control over production ￼. In short, “students don’t work for the inherent value of knowledge, but to earn a grade, and eventually a diploma”, which perfectly trains them to work for a paycheck rather than personal fulfillment ￼.

Bowles and Gintis highlight the “hidden curriculum” – the non-academic social lessons schools impart, such as punctuality, obedience, and acceptance of authority ￼. They cite evidence that personality traits like dependability and perseverance strongly predict student success (GPA) and are cultivated by schools, whereas traits like creativity or independence are often subtly discouraged ￼. The hidden curriculum thus manufactures the ideal “docile” worker under the guise of academic training ￼. Notably, Bowles and Gintis pointed out that large employers historically supported public education more for these non-cognitive effects (work habits, discipline) than for specific skills ￼. This aligns with Foucault’s notion of disciplinary power – schooling normalizes individuals to fit a social order.

Importantly, Bowles and Gintis also underscore how mass schooling was from the start a project intermingled with social control. They note that since the 19th century, reformers pursued twin goals: equality of opportunity on one hand, and social control of the lower classes on the other ￼. “Schooling has been at once something done to the poor and for the poor,” they write, with social control often winning out in practice ￼. For example, early public schools in the U.S. aimed to assimilate immigrants and inculcate a work ethic; in extreme cases like the Native American boarding schools, education was literally a tool of cultural eradication and control ￼. Bowles and Gintis observe that compulsory schooling was often undemocratic at its inception – it was imposed to solve social order problems (keeping youth off the streets, instilling Protestant industrial virtues) rather than out of purely democratic ideals ￼.

By bringing in Bowles & Gintis, we add a critical perspective: as education expanded to universality, it did so under economic imperatives that often contradict the lofty rhetoric of personal enlightenment. In the timeline of education, the 19th and 20th centuries’ massive expansion of schooling can be seen as driven by capitalism’s need for a disciplined, literate workforce and the state’s need for loyal, homogenized citizens ￼ ￼. This socio-economic engine helps explain why education became universalized (it wasn’t just benevolence – industrial societies required it). It also sets up a tension we will revisit: if the hidden curriculum trains compliance, what happens when new technologies (like personalized AI tutors) enter – do they reinforce the same compliance in a new guise, or disrupt the old correspondence?

Deleuze: Societies of Control and the Shift to Continuous Modulation

Gilles Deleuze, in his short essay Postscript on the Societies of Control (1990), builds on Foucault’s idea of disciplinary societies to argue that we have entered a new configuration of power – the control society. In disciplinary societies (per Foucault), people move through discrete enclosures: first the family, then school, then barracks, factory, maybe prison – each with its own strict rules and “molds” that shape individuals in each stage ￼. But Deleuze observes that in late 20th century, these molds were breaking down: institutions like the factory or school were in “continuous crisis” and being replaced by more fluid forms like corporations and lifelong training ￼. “In disciplinary societies you were always starting all over again… while in control societies you never finish anything,” notes Deleuze ￼. In other words, instead of the old progression (finish school, then separate phase: work, etc.), we now have perpetual education, training, and monitoring.

Two of Deleuze’s points are especially relevant to contemporary education:
	•	From enclosure to continuous control: “School is being replaced by continuing education and exams by continuous assessment. This is the surest way of turning education into a business.” ￼. Deleuze saw that instead of a person’s education ending at graduation, the modern ideal is lifelong learning – constantly updating skills, getting recertified, micro-credentialed, etc. Likewise, assessment moves from occasional high-stakes exams to ongoing, embedded monitoring (think of today’s learning apps that give instant quizzes, or workplaces requiring regular online training modules). This continuous assessment aligns with a neoliberal push to treat education as an “infinite upgrade” market. It also suggests that control is now exercised through freedom: you are “free” to learn anytime/anywhere, but in practice you are never done proving yourself. We will later see how AI-driven personalized platforms exemplify this – they keep the student always on some adaptive treadmill of content, generating data continuously, much like an employee being constantly evaluated.
	•	From individuals to “dividuals”: In control societies, according to Deleuze, people are no longer modeled as whole individuals within a mass, but as dividuals – data points in banks and systems ￼. He writes, “Individuals become dividuals, and masses become samples, data, markets, or banks.” ￼. This striking insight perfectly captures what happens when education becomes datafied. A student in a traditional class was an “individual” on the class roster (a name, a cumulative grade, a rank in class). A student in a fully digital learning system is a profile composed of many separate data parameters: a list of skills mastered, response times, clicks, engagement metrics, predicted knowledge states. The education system increasingly sees learners not as whole persons but as bundles of data to be continuously modulated (accelerated here, remediated there) by algorithmic systems. This ties into personalization: the learning experience is tailored, but only because the student has been decomposed into facets that the system can address (much as Amazon personalizes by treating you as a collection of past clicks and buys). Deleuze’s point is that control operates by adjusting these myriad parameters in real time (continuous modulation) rather than by imposing a one-size mold. We will later consider how learning analytics dashboards and adaptive tutors represent this shift – power through ubiquitous measurement and micro-adjustments rather than through coarse norms and punishments.

In sum, Deleuze’s society of control provides a conceptual bridge to discuss 21st-century education technology. It suggests that whereas a 19th-century schoolmaster disciplined by strict curriculum and corporal punishment, a 21st-century AI tutor “disciplines” by constant engagement, nudges, and data-driven feedback loops. The outcome may be a learner who appears freer (learning at their own pace, on their own path) yet is in some ways more tightly controlled than ever – every misstep captured, every strength or weakness fed back into the system to shape the next task. This is continuous control, and it’s the hallmark of how personalization can become the new “logic of control.”

With these frameworks established – Foucault’s historical epistemes and discipline, Baudrillard’s simulacra, Bowles & Gintis’s social reproduction, and Deleuze’s control society – we have a rich toolkit to analyze educational transformations. We can now proceed to trace the historical trajectory of education through these lenses.

Historical Trajectory: From Renaissance Learning to AI Education

Let us outline a timeline of education across major periods, highlighting how its nature and purpose shifted. We will integrate the perspectives above as we go:
	•	Pre-Modern & Renaissance Education (≈15th–17th centuries): In the Renaissance episteme, knowledge was seen as uncovering God’s order through resemblances and symbols ￼. Education was an elite affair, typically for clergymen, scholars, or courtiers. Universities existed (since medieval times) but served a tiny fraction of the population, mostly to reproduce the clergy or learned professions (law, medicine). Teaching relied on classical texts and faith; theology was “queen of the sciences.” We might call this education as illumination – aiming to reflect a higher reality (religious or classical truth). Discipline in this era was largely religious/corporate (monastic rules in universities). Baudrillard’s first-order simulacrum applies: a lecture or text was taken as a faithful copy of truth, and a scholar’s knowledge was assumed to correspond to reality (e.g. knowing Aristotle’s philosophy meant you “knew” how the world works, in their view). There was little concept of mass schooling; most children learned via apprenticeship or informal family transmission, not via a state curriculum.
	•	Enlightenment and Post-Enlightenment Classical Era (≈18th century): As the Classical episteme took hold, rationality and order became key. Philosophes like Locke, Rousseau, and later Kant wrote on education, often emphasizing reason, individual development, and secular civics. This period saw first moves toward secular public education – for example, the late 18th century Prussian reforms which introduced state-funded compulsory primary schooling (one of the first states to do so in 1763) ￼. The goal was partly enlightened (literate, informed citizenry) and partly utilitarian (trained soldiers and bureaucrats) ￼ ￼. By the early 19th century, Prussia’s model included graded schools, trained teachers, national curricula and examinations (the Abitur) ￼ ￼ – a template that influenced other countries (e.g. Horace Mann importing ideas to the U.S.). During this epoch, education began shifting from the few to the many, but still not fully universal (e.g. in England and France, compulsory schooling laws came only in the 1880s ￼). Foucault’s classical episteme meant categorization – schools started sorting students by age, ability, etc., introducing the idea of “normal” progress. The nation-state realized the value of schooling for instilling national identity and social order. As one education historian noted, “the ambition of universal literacy in Europe was a fundamental reform born of the Enlightenment” ￼, but it took long to achieve. Indeed, literacy rates in Europe rose from very low in 17th century to perhaps 50% by mid-19th century in some countries ￼ ￼ – a slow climb until accelerating later. Baudrillard’s second stage simulacrum is nascent here: schooling purported to be about enlightenment, yet as soon as states got involved, schooling became entangled with propaganda and control (Napoleonic France’s schools teaching obedience to the state, etc.). Still, reality and representation were closely linked – you learned tangible skills (reading, writing) deemed essential to “civilized” reality.
	•	Industrial & Modern Era (19th – early 20th centuries): This is the era when education becomes a mass system in the West. Driven by the Industrial Revolution and nationalist competition, most developed countries established nationwide public school systems by the late 19th or early 20th century. For example, the United States saw common schools spread in the mid-1800s, and by the early 20th century high school enrollment boomed. The impetus was both economic (industries needed a basically literate, punctual, and compliant workforce) and political (forge a national culture, “Americanize” immigrants, etc.). Foucault’s disciplinary techniques reached full bloom: schools with ringing bells, standard schedules, inspection visits, report cards – all aimed at instilling habits. Bowles & Gintis’s analysis squarely fits here: “since the mid-nineteenth century the dual objectives of educational reformers – equality of opportunity and social control – have been intermingled” ￼, with schooling often serving to legitimate inequality while appearing to level the field. Indeed, the late 19th-century school system largely transmitted existing class positions (only a minority went to college, others tracked into vocational paths). Students were taught a “hidden curriculum” of punctuality, respect for authority, and acceptance of boredom – all necessary for factory and office life ￼ ￼. Yet the ideology of the time celebrated education as the great equalizer (the Horatio Alger myth, etc.), a necessary facade to maintain social order ￼. Baudrillard’s second-order simulacrum is clearly present: the school system masked and denatured reality ￼ by claiming to reward merit while in fact reproducing class structures (for instance, wealthy districts had better schools, child laborers and minorities received inferior schooling). Still, there was a strong belief that the curriculum (the 3 R’s, patriotic history, etc.) corresponded to useful reality – in other words, education was considered instrumental to progress (learn science to become an engineer; learn civics to be a good citizen). Education at this stage straddled a line: it was genuine knowledge transfer (literacy, basic skills are very real) but also social myth (school as the path to success for all, which was not statistically true due to systemic barriers).
	•	High Modernism & Mid-20th Century (Post-WWII): After World War II, education in developed countries expanded unprecedentedly – the high school became universal, and college attendance surged (the GI Bill in the US, etc.). This era, roughly 1950s–1970s, saw schooling become a mass bureaucracy. The curriculum standardized nationwide (e.g. emergence of standardized testing regimes, SAT/ACT, etc.), and education was drawn into Cold War competition (science education push after Sputnik). Episteme-wise, one might say this is late Modern or even transitional to Postmodern: faith in science and expertise was high, yet critical questioning of institutions also grew in the 1960s. From Foucault’s view, this period is perhaps the zenith of the “exam society” – everything from IQ tests to college entrance exams and tracking tests proliferated, fitting his idea that the examination combines power and knowledge to classify individuals. Bowles & Gintis note that by this time, “the unequal contest between social control and social justice is evident in U.S. education’s total functioning” ￼ – meaning that despite more egalitarian access, schools still systematically favored those from higher socio-economic status (the correlation between parents’ income and children’s educational attainment remained very strong ￼). Baudrillard’s third-order simulacrum increasingly applies here: credentials multiply (high school is no longer enough; one needs a college degree, then maybe a graduate degree), and they start to “mask the absence of reality” ￼ in the sense that inflation of credentials requires everyone to get more schooling for the same jobs. The signal (diploma) becomes more important than the knowledge itself. Sociologists in the 1970s (like Randall Collins) spoke of the credential society, where education is more about showing a badge than about skills. We can see the hyperreal creeping in: e.g., a person spends four years in college largely to obtain a piece of paper that signifies competence, whether or not those years of study truly added practical value.
	•	Postmodern Era and the Rise of Standardized Simulation (late 20th century): By the 1980s–2000s, educational discourse adopted both market logic and data-driven accountability. Neoliberal policies pushed school choice, competition, and audit culture (school rankings, test score-based funding). A critical turning point was the idea (especially in the U.S. post-A Nation At Risk report, 1983) that schools were failing and needed to be measured and managed. This led to the era of No Child Left Behind and PISA exams, where endless testing and metrics became normal. In philosophical terms, this is late postmodern: skepticism about grand narratives (so less emphasis on broad humanist education) and focus on performative outcomes. The Baudrillardian simulacrum now clearly third-order: test scores, evaluation rubrics, and college rankings create a simulated reality of educational quality. A school becomes “good” if it scores well on metrics – a perfect example of a sign that masks whether real learning is happening. The map (school rating) might entirely misrepresent the territory (true educational enrichment), yet policy and parental choices treat the map as real. In classrooms, teaching to standardized tests often meant simulating proficiency – drilling students on how to answer expected questions, even as genuine critical thinking might be sidelined. We see here education functioning as what Baudrillard called “a game of truth” under a new regime – e.g., under high-stakes accountability, the “truth” of learning is whatever shows up in the data, and teachers feel compelled to produce those numbers. Foucault might say a new regime of truth was instituted: “scientifically-based education research” and test metrics ruled, marginalizing other ways of thinking about teaching ￼. By 2000, a student’s digital record and scores were beginning to define them more than direct observation or qualitative feedback – a precursor to the full datafication to come.
	•	21st Century – AI and Datafication (Postmodern/AI Epoch): Entering the last two decades, we step fully into what we’ve called the fourth-order simulacrum of education. Several trends define this period: digitization of learning, personalization algorithms, big data analytics, and AI tutoring. The classroom is no longer the sole locus of learning – instead, learning is expected to be lifelong, on-demand, and mediated by technology. Learning management systems (LMS) and learning analytics track student clicks and progress in real time; adaptive learning software adjusts content on the fly; and most recently, AI chatbots like GPT-4 are beginning to serve as tutors or teaching aides. Underlying all this is a new episteme or worldview: one that sees information as abundant and the key task as filtering and guiding each individual through a personalized pathway (often framed in terms of efficiency and optimization). There is a strong faith in data – a sense that if we just collect enough data about how each student learns, we can algorithmically customize education to maximize outcomes. This could be seen as an emerging episteme of “algorithmic truth” in education: rather than philosophical or scientific truth, the guiding principle is whatever pattern the data asserts (e.g., if data mining finds that doing X leads to score gains, X becomes “what works,” regardless of theory). Education becomes a domain of continuous feedback loops, fitting Deleuze’s description of a control society.

In terms of Baudrillard, many aspects of current education are arguably hyperreal simulations: for instance, students on adaptive platforms often experience a gamified environment – accumulating points, badges, leveling up skills (a direct simulation of a video game, applied to learning). The knowledge content is packaged in engaging multimedia, possibly far removed from any original context – learning algebra through a fantasy game world, etc., which can be great pedagogy, but also means the learning experience is highly mediated. A striking example of simulacrum is MOOCs (Massive Open Online Courses) and online degree programs: universities clone their courses into digital platforms so anyone can “attend” – yet the experience of, say, watching video lectures and doing auto-graded quizzes is a simulation of the college experience, often without the interactions and mentorship that constitute the real education for on-campus students. The MOOC credential may carry less weight – an implicit recognition that it’s a copy without the full original – though this might change as simulation improves.

Furthermore, the personalization narrative often claims to be more “reality-aligned” for the student (meeting them where they are), but it introduces new layers of abstraction: the student is interacting with an AI that represents “the knowledge domain.” For example, a system like Knewton (in its heyday) would model a student’s proficiency on dozens of micro-concepts and select content accordingly ￼ ￼. To the student, the tutor in the computer feels omniscient, “like a robot tutor in the sky that can read your mind” ￼, but the truth is this AI is manipulating symbols and probabilities, not genuinely understanding in the human sense. Still, if the student and teacher both accept the AI’s recommendations as truth (“the data says you’re 80% mastery on quadratic equations, so we’ll review that”), the data model becomes the reality. In Foucault’s terms, a new “ensemble of rules for the production of truth” has arrived ￼: what the dashboard shows is what’s true about the student’s learning. This is a new regime of truth in education – one driven by algorithms and analytics.

Do they truly personalize and empower, or do they reinforce existing power dynamics under a new veneer? And importantly, what are the implications for equity and freedom?

Case Studies: AI-Powered Education Technologies and Their Significance

In the past decade, numerous EdTech innovations have promised to “revolutionize” education. We will consider a few prominent examples – Knewton, DreamBox, Khan Academy (mastery learning & Khanmigo AI), and the emergence of GPT-style tutors – and analyze them through our theoretical lenses.

Knewton: Big-Data Adaptive Learning as Hyperreality

Knewton was one of the earliest and loudest proponents of big data in education. Founded in 2008, it built an adaptive learning engine that could integrate with digital textbooks and courses. Knewton’s approach was to gather millions of data points on student interactions and use algorithms to deliver personalized content – famously described by its CEO Jose Ferreira as “a robot tutor in the sky that can semi-read your mind and figure out what your strengths and weaknesses are, down to the percentile.” ￼ This colorful metaphor reveals both the promise and the hyperbole of the system.

In Foucauldian terms, Knewton represents a new mode of power-knowledge in education: instead of a human teacher observing and judging a student, an algorithmic system observes (via data) and judges (via predictive analytics) the student’s knowledge state. It creates a profile (a kind of digital truth) about the student. This can be empowering – each student gets custom exercises at their level – but it also bypasses the student’s own self-knowledge and the teacher’s expertise, putting faith in the algorithmic gaze. The regime of truth here is the quantitative performance data: if Knewton’s dashboard says you are 60% proficient in Algebraic Fractions, that becomes the reality to act upon. The student is nudged along a path that Knewton deems optimal based on patterns from “millions of students … just like you” ￼. This is essentially Deleuze’s control society in action – “continuous control” via an endless stream of tasks and adjustments, replacing the old periodic exams ￼.

Beneath the utopian promise (“mind-reading tutor!”), one can see Baudrillard’s simulacrum. The system simulates the role of a personal tutor – traditionally an elite privilege – and makes it infinitely scalable. However, does it truly understand the student? Not in a human way; it’s correlating responses. It’s a carefully crafted illusion of a tutor, arguably a third-order simulacrum: it pretends to be a faithful teaching experience, but what it really does is mold the student’s behavior to fit the system’s model. A student might learn to click the right answers through trial and error without deeply understanding, yet the system will “think” the concept is mastered. In this way, the sign of mastery (completing Knewton’s sequence) might mask a shallow or absent mastery – a potential hyperreality of learning. Ferreira’s own boast that Knewton could “semi-read your mind” ￼ acknowledges this is a simulation of understanding.

Interestingly, the outcome for Knewton as a company was sobering – despite the hype, it struggled to produce consistently great results and was eventually sold off in 2019 ￼. This suggests that while the idea was ahead of its time, the execution perhaps fell into the trap of over-simplifying education as data problem – the human elements of motivation, curiosity, etc., are hard to quantify. Nonetheless, Knewton blazed a trail, and its legacy is seen in virtually all modern EdTech which now routinely use recommendation algorithms and knowledge tracing.

Key insight: Knewton embodies the shift where the process of education becomes more opaque to learners and teachers, and more transparent to machines. The machine sees the data and adapts, while the learner may not even know why they are being given a particular exercise (the rationale is hidden in the algorithm). This can deskill teachers (who must trust the system) and render students somewhat passive (following the personalized script). It is a clear example of personalization as a new form of control – subtle and individualized, but control nonetheless.

DreamBox: Gamified Adaptive Learning and the Hidden Curriculum 2.0

DreamBox Learning is another adaptive learning platform, focused on K-8 mathematics. It provides an “Intelligent Adaptive Learning™” environment that is highly gamified and student-driven. DreamBox continuously collects data points as a child works through math games and puzzles, and it adjusts the difficulty and provides scaffolding or enrichment in real time ￼ ￼. The interface is colorful and game-like, aiming to keep children engaged. DreamBox also generates detailed reports for teachers and parents, translating the child’s gameplay into progress on learning standards ￼.

From a theoretical angle, DreamBox highlights the blend of engagement and control characteristic of 21st-century EdTech. The child experiences a sense of play (freedom, fun), but under the hood an algorithm is guiding every step – a sweetened form of Foucault’s observation that power is tolerable only if it masks a substantial part of itself; here, discipline is masked as a game. Deleuze’s dividual is evident: each student’s interaction is sliced into metrics – which strategy they used, how long they paused, which mistakes they made – all fed into the adaptation logic. The teacher sees the student not solely as “Johnny in my class who struggles with fractions” but as a dashboard of bar graphs. This arguably creates a new “gaze” in education: not the panoptic gaze of the teacher overseeing the class, but the data gaze of the software and the stakeholders reading its analytics.

From a Baudrillard perspective, DreamBox could be seen as a controlled second-order simulacrum moving to third. It still has the veneer of actual math learning (the games do teach math concepts), but it also creates an alternate reality of rewards and narratives to motivate the child. One might ask: is the child mastering math, or mastering DreamBox? When a student solves a problem in DreamBox, the context is often fantastical (like feeding a virtual dinosaur by solving equations); the fear is that this context is so self-contained that the knowledge might not transfer to the real world (a form of hyperreality where within the game the math makes sense, but outside it might not). However, DreamBox’s advocates would argue the conceptual understanding does carry over. This tension – learning vs. simulation of learning – is at the heart of EdTech efficacy debates.

Another notable aspect: DreamBox’s CEO has emphasized equity, claiming to “unlock the learning potential of every child regardless of zip code” ￼. This speaks to the hope that personalization might mitigate inequities by giving each child the tailored support that well-off kids might get from tutors. It’s a noble goal (and one Foucauldians might applaud as a democratization of the tutor-student power relation). Yet, a critical view asks if this is just ideology: Does DreamBox truly close gaps, or do affluent schools use it as enrichment while poorer schools use it as a substitute for teachers? Initial independent studies show mixed results. Bowles & Gintis might note that even with such tools, if the broader social context (poverty, underfunded schools) isn’t addressed, the playing field remains uneven.

In essence, DreamBox showcases personalized learning as a friendly, seemingly liberated experience that nonetheless exemplifies the control society: “modulation” instead of strict mold ￼. Each student is on a different lesson sequence, but all are funnelled through the program’s design. They have agency to an extent (choosing which game or when to take a hint), yet the system’s logic ultimately decides their trajectory. It’s individualized and standardized at once – a hallmark of how technology reconciles mass education with personalization.

Khan Academy and Mastery Learning: Data-Driven Mastery and GPT Integration

Khan Academy is a widely used platform that started with video lessons and evolved into a comprehensive learning system with exercises and mastery-based progression. In Khan Academy’s Mastery Learning system, students practice skills and must achieve a certain proficiency (through quizzes and unit tests) to mark a skill as “Mastered.” They can progress at their own pace, which is a revival of an older educational ideal (mastery learning was popularized by Bloom in the 1960s) but now turbocharged by technology. Dashboards allow teachers to see each student’s status – e.g., which skills are mastered, which need work – enabling targeted intervention.

Khan Academy exemplifies an interesting hybrid of Foucauldian discipline and control. On one hand, it liberates learners from the rigid pace of a grade-level classroom; on the other, it imposes a strict criterion for mastery (often 80% or 90% success on problems) and monitors every practice attempt. There is a strong normalizing judgment: the system defines what it means to know algebra, and students get categorized (fluent, needs practice, etc.) accordingly. However, unlike a one-time exam, the system encourages continuous retakes and practice, aligning with Deleuze’s notion that in control societies one “never finishes” learning – you are expected to continuously improve and fill all gaps ￼.

Importantly, Khan Academy has recently integrated AI through a tool called Khanmigo, powered by GPT-4. Khanmigo functions as a tutor that can converse with students in natural language, helping them through problems with Socratic hints, and as an assistant for teachers (e.g., generating quiz questions or lesson summaries). Khan Academy’s leadership described GPT-4 as “opening up new frontiers in education… transformative” ￼, but with caution to use it responsibly. Khanmigo is explicitly designed not to give answers outright, but to guide learners with questions like a human tutor might ￼.

From our theoretical angle, Khanmigo and similar GPT tutors represent the cutting edge of simulation in education. A GPT-4 tutor is literally a simulation of a human tutor – trained on vast data, it can mimic explanations, ask follow-up questions, even inject a bit of empathy (“I see you’re struggling with this step, let’s try a different approach”). This is Baudrillard’s hyperreal in potent form: interacting with Khanmigo can feel uncannily like a real tutoring session, yet one is talking to no one, essentially conversing with a statistically constructed model of language. The pedagogical value can be high (imagine a student who cannot afford a human tutor now getting 24/7 help). But we must ask: what is lost in the simulacrum? For one, current AI sometimes errs or produces plausible-sounding nonsense. Khan Academy acknowledges this, using humans to monitor and correct AI outputs during the pilot ￼ ￼. But beyond factual accuracy, there’s the subtler issue of human connection – a human tutor can inspire and care in ways an AI cannot fully simulate (at least as of now). If education is not just transmission of content but also about mentorship, identity formation, and emotional support, then relying on AI tutors could flatten the experience to its cognitive dimension only. That said, perhaps future AI will get better at emotional labor too – raising ethical questions about attachment to machine personas.

For Foucault, one might consider how GPT-based tutors fit into the power-knowledge schema. On one hand, they democratize knowledge (anyone can ask anything, no embarrassment). On the other, the AI is a creation of certain corporate and ideological processes – it embodies the knowledge (and biases) present in its training data. There is a risk of a new kind of authority: students might trust the AI implicitly (after all, it’s very confident and doesn’t scold you), so the “regime of truth” could shift such that “It’s true because the AI said so.” That could undermine critical thinking if not carefully managed. Educators will need to teach students to question AI as they would any source.

Khan Academy’s integration of GPT also underscores the theme of personalization as control. Every Khanmigo interaction can be logged and analyzed (though Khan is non-profit and likely careful with data, the point stands generally). The student gets personalized support, but also personalized surveillance in effect – if an AI can detect you’re struggling, it could potentially alert teachers or adjust your difficulty. Over time, as AI tutors become mainstream, one can envisage a system where every student’s entire learning dialogue is recorded. This is a panopticon on steroids – Bentham’s prison saw the possibility of constant observation, but AI tutors will actually engage constantly while also observing. Foucault might ask: how does this internalize discipline? A student might become highly self-conscious that “the system knows all my weak points,” potentially reducing willingness to take intellectual risks (or maybe increasing it if the environment feels safe – it can cut both ways).

In summary, Khan Academy’s mastery learning and AI tutor initiatives highlight the potential for a positive synthesis – a form of education that is mastery-based (addressing each student’s needs) and richly interactive (conversations with AI). It arguably pushes back on some aspects of the hyperreal assembly-line schooling by re-centering on mastery rather than seat-time. However, it simultaneously intensifies datafication and continuous monitoring, advancing the control society in education. Whether this balance results in more authentic learning or a slicker simulation of learning will depend on implementation and context.

GPT-4 and other AI Tutors in the Wild

Beyond Khan Academy, we are seeing an explosion of AI tutoring apps and integrations. Startups boast GPT-powered math solvers, essay feedback tools, language conversation partners, etc. For example, apps like Duolingo are integrating GPT-4 to allow free-form conversation practice in foreign languages, effectively simulating a native speaker for the learner. There are also AI-powered virtual teaching assistants that help lighten teachers’ load by answering student questions in discussion forums (already in use in some online university courses).

These developments raise a common theme: the simulation of human educational roles. The teacher, tutor, or study partner can now be software. In many respects, this is a fulfillment of the classic vision of “education technology”: even in the 1960s, pioneers like Patrick Suppes imagined every student having a computer-based tutor. But Baudrillard might caution: when the simulation becomes too perfect, do we lose sight of what was being simulated? If a student can get an answer or explanation from an AI in seconds, perhaps they won’t struggle through the productive confusion that sometimes leads to deeper insight. If an AI writes “good job, I see your reasoning” with a smiling emoji, does that carry the same encouragement as a teacher’s praise? Some studies (pre-GPT era) found students can form emotional bonds with even simple conversational agents. We are headed into an era of “hyperpersonalization” where not only is the content tailored, but the persona delivering it might be tailored (imagine choosing your AI tutor’s voice, gender, teaching style). This could greatly benefit engagement, yet it is literally education in the mirror of our own desires – a simulation tuned to please us, which is different from education challenging us with otherness.

From a power viewpoint, AI tutors could either democratize access (everyone gets a personal Oxford-level tutor avatar) or exacerbate divides (those with the best AI or ability to use it get further ahead). Consider equity: if wealthy students use AI tutors to essentially homeschool themselves into mastery (or do their homework instantly), while poor students are in overcrowded classrooms with no such tools, inequality could widen. On the other hand, those same tools could reach under-served areas cheaply via smartphones. It’s a double-edged sword.

Bowles & Gintis would surely ask: does AI tutoring tackle the structural inequalities or just provide a high-tech bandage? They might note that AI will likely be used to further the “business-ification” of education (as Deleuze said, turning education into a business ￼). Already, major tech companies view the education market as huge. If AI allows personalized learning at scale, one could educate many with fewer human teachers – which sounds efficient, but also threatens teacher jobs and the de-professionalization of teaching. Teachers may be relegated to facilitators or monitors of AI systems, rather than content experts or mentors, potentially weakening the labor power of educators (something a Marxian view would be wary of).

One can also view AI tutors as the ultimate extension of the “correspondence principle”: they prepare students even more directly for a workplace where your boss might be an algorithm. If schooling becomes a process of interacting with AI and being ranked by data, that nicely corresponds to many modern workplaces (gig economy managed by apps, etc.). This alignment could mean schooling is indeed reproducing the next stage of capitalism, where control is algorithmic and workers must learn to optimize themselves to please the metrics.

New Regimes of Truth and Control in the Datafied Classroom

As we aggregate these observations, a clear picture emerges: education’s digital transformation is not just a technological shift, but an epistemic and political one. There is a new truth regime taking shape: one that centers on data, algorithms, and predictive analytics as the arbiters of educational reality. Education researcher Ben Williamson calls this the “datafication of education,” where it’s assumed that quantifying learning yields objective, actionable truth ￼. For example, a learning analytics system might predict a student’s risk of failing a course by analyzing login frequency and quiz scores – that prediction can become a self-fulfilling truth if everyone (teachers, student, advisors) believes and acts on it.

The concept of “regimes of truth” (Foucault) is useful here: historically, schools were part of the regime of truth that centered on academic knowledge and teacher expertise. Now, AI and analytics constitute a competing regime: what the computer says about a student might override the teacher’s judgment or the student’s self-assessment. In some cases, we see the rise of automated decision-making: e.g., AI-driven systems that decide when a student is ready to move on, or even AI systems used in university admissions to filter applicants. This represents a shift in governance – call it algorithmic governmentality – where power operates through the continuous monitoring and micro-decision-making by code.

The Panopticon metaphor is often invoked for digital surveillance, and it fits to an extent: students under analytics are visible in detail to those with access to the dashboards (administrators, companies, etc.). However, unlike Bentham’s Panopticon, where one central watchman observes all, here observation is distributed and also partially opaque (students often don’t know what data is collected or how it’s used). This asymmetry raises concerns: who owns the data? EdTech companies often do. We thus see a power shift: tech firms becoming influential actors in shaping curricula and student behavior via their platforms. The “gaze” in education is increasingly a commercial algorithmic gaze.

Another Foucauldian concept, the “exam”, has mutated: the whole learning process becomes an exam (continuous assessment) ￼. Instead of a final test determining your fate, every interaction contributes to a rolling evaluation. This could reduce pressure (no single high-stakes moment) but increase constant anxiety or complacency (“the system will auto-correct me if I slack”). Foucault also talked about the normation process – defining a norm and trying to bring everyone toward it. In datafied education, norms are constantly recalculated (class average on a dashboard, percentile ranks updated in real-time). The student is always measured against dynamic norms, potentially internalizing an endless race.

On Baudrillard’s side, the concept of hyperreality encourages us to ask: at what point do educational experiences become so simulated that they lose sight of any original “real” education? Consider virtual reality (VR) learning: one can dissect a frog in VR or explore ancient Rome in VR. Wonderful opportunities, but also a step further into experiencing the sign (the virtual frog) as if it were reality. If a student only dissects virtually, have they learned the same biology as a hands-on lab would teach, or have they learned a simulation? In many cases, the simulation may be good enough or even safer/better – but it’s a philosophical question of value. The danger is if appearance overtakes substance: e.g., a school might implement flashy personalized learning software and see test scores tick up, proclaiming success, while students actually engage in fewer meaningful discussions or creative projects. The education system may come to value what is easily measurable and simulatable over what is not (like empathy, critical thinking, collaboration in person). This aligns with what some scholars call the “performativity” in education – valuing the performance indicators above all.

Yet, to avoid a doom-and-gloom stance: there is also the possibility that these new technologies, used wisely, could actually bring education closer to its ideals. Mastery learning and formative feedback are proven effective practices; AI and analytics might enable them at scale in ways not previously feasible. The personalization of pace and path could free students from the frustration of one-size-fits-all, which has been a major flaw in traditional schooling (and often to the detriment of both the quickest and slowest learners). It could also free teachers from drudgery (AI taking over grading of routine assignments, giving teachers more time for human-centric mentorship).

The crucial question becomes one of control and agency: Will learners and educators be the ones guiding these tools, or will the tools (and their corporate/political owners) guide the educational process autonomously? In an ideal scenario, AI serves as a transparent aid – a servant to human-designed pedagogy. In a dystopian scenario, AI and data regimes could marginalize teacher expertise, enforce standardization (paradoxically through personalization, by pushing everyone toward predefined objectives), and commodify every learning moment (“learning as profit-generating data”).

Implications for Equity, Freedom, and Policy

The final part of our inquiry is to reflect on what these transformations mean for fundamental values: equity, freedom, and how policy should respond.

Equity: Education has long been seen as the great equalizer, but also critiqued as reinforcing inequality. The current trends are equally double-edged. On one hand, AI tutors and open online courses can in theory bring top-quality instruction to anyone with an internet connection. A student in a remote village can access lectures from MIT, or get math help at midnight from a chatbot. This democratization is real and important – many learners who lacked resources can now learn nearly any subject online (witness the popularity of Khan Academy in developing countries). Furthermore, adaptive systems can help close gaps within a classroom: a student who is behind can get remediation instead of being left to fail, and an advanced student can be challenged more, addressing the “teach to the middle” problem ￼.

On the other hand, new inequities could emerge: access to technology is not uniform (the digital divide means some have devices and broadband, others not). There’s also a risk of a two-tier education system: affluent families may use technology to enhance learning (e.g., hiring both a human tutor and using AI tools, choosing the best tech-rich schools), while less privileged communities might be subjected to algorithmic, teacher-less learning as a cost-saving measure. For instance, some underfunded school systems might be tempted to replace instructors with software for practice drills, whereas wealthier schools keep low student-teacher ratios and use software only as supplement. This echoes a fear that personalization could be used to justify cutting human support for those who need it most – a perverse outcome where those kids get “robots instead of teachers” under the guise of innovation.

Bias in AI is another equity concern. If the algorithms guiding learning are trained on data that reflects societal biases, they might, for example, unwittingly steer certain students (say, based on demographic patterns) away from advanced topics (“based on your performance, maybe you should try a simpler track”) or penalize dialects and phrasing in automated essay scoring that differ from the mainstream. Ensuring fairness and transparency in these systems is a huge policy challenge.

Freedom: Education in a democracy should prepare individuals for freedom – for independent thought and civic participation. There is a worry that the hyper-structured, surveilled nature of personalized learning might undermine that mission. If students grow up with their entire educational journey tracked and optimized, they might internalize the idea that there is always a correct path, determined by an external system. This could reduce the freedom to err and wander, which is often crucial for creativity and self-discovery. Additionally, privacy is a freedom issue: students (and their parents) may not have meaningful consent over data collection. Profiles built in K-12 could follow someone to college admissions or job hiring – perhaps feeding into algorithms used by those institutions (this is speculative but technically possible as data linking grows). There’s a dystopian scenario where a “learning score” or behavioral record from school AI systems predestines certain opportunities or outcomes, constraining life choices – a kind of social credit system via education.

That said, personalized learning can increase freedom in the micro sense: freedom to choose what to learn (interest-based pathways), freedom to take breaks from a rigid schedule, etc. A student deeply interested in coding could, with online resources, go far beyond what their school offers – that intellectual freedom is enhanced by technology. Also, being freed from the fear of failing publicly (since adaptive systems can be private) might embolden some students to explore more difficult material – freedom from humiliation, as it were, which traditional classrooms sometimes inadvertently cause.

Policy implications: Policymakers are confronted with balancing innovation and safeguards. Some areas to consider:
	•	Data governance: Strong policies on student data privacy, security, and ownership are needed. For instance, laws should perhaps ensure that student data from learning platforms cannot be sold or used for advertising, and that students have the right to have their data deleted or to opt out of purely algorithmic decision-making. Several jurisdictions have begun this (e.g., EU’s GDPR and some US state laws focus on student data privacy).
	•	Transparency and Auditability: If algorithms are determining educational pathways or high-stakes evaluations, there should be transparency in how they work (at least to expert auditors) and the ability to appeal or override. A “black box” AI should not have final say on a student’s tracking or college entry without human review.
	•	Maintaining Human Role: Policies and standards could emphasize that AI is a tool for teachers, not a replacement. Investment in training teachers to use these tools is crucial – a teacher augmented by AI (for routine tasks) could have more time for one-on-one mentoring and the affective side of teaching. If policy goes the other way (using AI to reduce teacher numbers to save cost), the quality and human touch of education will suffer.
	•	Digital Equity: Public investment to ensure all students/schools have access to the infrastructure (devices, internet) and to high-quality digital curricula is needed, or technology will widen gaps. Additionally, support for parents and communities to understand and engage with these new systems is important so that education doesn’t become something happening in a silo between child and machine, but stays a communal, supported endeavor.
	•	Curriculum and Critical Thinking: There may be a need to update curricula to include critical digital literacy – teaching students about AI and algorithms, so they better understand the tools they are using (and don’t treat them as infallible oracles). This empowers students to use AI wisely and ethically.
	•	Ethical guidelines: Education technology should be guided by ethical frameworks (some orgs have begun issuing AI ethics in education guidelines). These might cover avoiding bias, ensuring inclusion (AI should be tested to work well for different kinds of learners, including those with disabilities or learning differences), and promoting pedagogical soundness (not just engagement metrics).

In crafting policy, a Foucauldian might remind us to ask: cui bono? Who benefits from these new systems? We should be wary if the answer is primarily “tech vendors and data brokers” rather than students and educators. A Baudrillardian might suggest we also ask: are we preserving the “real” in education or only enhancing the simulation? Policies that encourage experiential learning, social-emotional learning, and other irreducibly human aspects can ensure that amidst all the screen time and AI, students still engage with the physical world and each other in meaningful ways.

Conclusion

The journey from the monkish scholars of the Renaissance to the AI-powered learners of today is nothing short of astonishing. Education has been continuously reconstituted: as a site of knowledge, it has mirrored each era’s way of understanding the world, and as a social institution, it has embodied each era’s power structures and ambitions. By applying Foucault’s and Baudrillard’s lenses, we unearth the deep logic beneath these shifts. We see that what is taught, how it’s taught, and why it’s taught have all been contingent on historical epistemes and that the classroom has always been a microcosm of larger social controls – whether the overt discipline of the 19th-century schoolhouse or the soft, invisible guidance of a learning algorithm.

In our present moment, education stands at another crossroads. The fourth-order simulacrum that Baudrillard warned of – where reality and appearance blur – is increasingly palpable in education, as virtual learning, gamification, and AI tutors create experiences that may feel as effective as “real” learning, yet we must constantly probe and ensure there is substance behind the appearance. Meanwhile, the society of control, as Deleuze described, is evident in how education is becoming an unending flow of assessment and personalization, promising liberation from one-size-fits-all schooling but introducing new modes of surveillance and normalization.

What gives hope is that understanding these forces equips us to make intentional choices. We can choose to harness data and AI in service of genuine learning and equity, rather than efficiency or profit alone. We can insist that the human elements of education – curiosity, creativity, social interaction, critical dialogue – not be sacrificed at the altar of optimization. The works of Foucault remind us that every system of power produces resistance; indeed, we see pushback against hyper-measurement in movements for more holistic education, or student digital privacy rights. Baudrillard reminds us to treasure what’s real and not be fully seduced by the hyperreal; in education, that might mean ensuring students still get hands-on experiments, face-to-face debates, and chances to deal with ambiguity that no app can fully script.

In closing, the evolution of education from an elite privilege to a ubiquitous simulator of social reality is a story of entanglement of enlightenment and control. Each expansion of access and knowledge has come with new techniques to manage minds. As we enter an era of intelligent machines as educators, we must navigate this entanglement with care. The ideal outcome would be a system where AI and data amplify the best of human teaching and learning – making it more accessible, personalized, and effective – while robust human values and oversight keep the system oriented toward truth, emancipation, and human dignity. Achieving that will require interdisciplinary wisdom: philosophical insight into what education is for, technical expertise to design ethical tools, and the voices of educators, students, and communities to ground policy in lived reality.

Education has always been a normative project – about shaping future society. Thus, the question before us is: what kind of society do we encode into our educational simulacra? If we consciously embed equity, critical thinking, and humanity into the algorithms, then the new simulations need not be sinister; they can be the next chapter in the humanist dream of enlightenment, albeit realized through new media. In the words of Foucault, “it’s not a matter of emancipating truth from every system of power (which would be an illusion, for truth is already power) but of detaching the power of truth from the forms of hegemony” ￼ ￼. Applying this to our context: we cannot simply remove power from education (education is power), but we can strive to ensure the new truths generated in education by AI/data serve learners rather than dominate them. And as Baudrillard might challenge us – we should remain vigilant that in the chase for improvement, we do not lose the meaning of what is being improved. The map must not replace the territory; the simulation, in the end, must point back to and enrich the reality of a student’s mind and society.

By understanding where we came from and the logics at play, we stand a better chance of steering education toward a future that is both technologically advanced and profoundly humane – a future in which the “universal simulation” of education fulfills the promise of universal enlightenment rather than merely universal control. The task is difficult but vital: as long as education remains a site of hope, we must continuously interrogate and guide its transformations so that the liberation in learning outpaces the regulation, and the reality beneath our educational simulacra remains rich and empowering.
