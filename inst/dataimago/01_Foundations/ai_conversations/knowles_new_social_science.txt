The idea of New Social Science (NSS) is grandiose, but is proposed as a series of tangible projects that demonstrate pieces of the whole. *   Editing and refining the tracks
    
*   Selecting and organizing the best tracks into an album
    
*   Packaging and finalizing the album
    
*   Developing marketing materials to promote the album
    
*   Teasing the album release with some promotional material
    
*   Album release and celebration
    
*   Post album "tour" and support
    
This cycle is important because the goal of NSS is not to do cool things, but to build an audience and support for a new way of doing things. In a word, to build fans. ### **Beyond Albums**
The album metaphor has its limits. The decisive difference for NSS is that each album is not a finished work, but an example which it is hoped others will copy, extend, and revise. They will provide a template and examples and invite others to make their own "branches" or "forks" to revise the work or extend it in new directions. Each "album" will need to demonstrate examples for this as well. Not fully worked revisions and branches, but rather small demonstrations of potential changes and how users can implement them.
## **Background and Vision**
The motivating idea behind NSS is that social scientists are unaccountable positivists. That is, they claim the purpose is identifying theories and evaluating them on their predictive power, but this never happens in practice. The truth is social science models and theories are too often insular, non-reproducible, idiosyncratic snapshots of specific social conditions and only those conditions that produce novel or positive results generate published research.
NSS does not seek to supplant this work, but to exist alongside (and possibly inform and assist it) by focusing on _description_ and **prediction**. For a fuller description see the section on NSS below. The purpose here is to **build knowledge** and encode that knowledge in ways that is easy for other researchers to pick up and start from. Basic facts, basic patterns, and basic trends should all be encoded in data and code and made easily available to anyone seeking to understand the social world. Then, the work of social science can be shifted from constantly starting from an unknown basline, and instead become an additive project speaking from a common set of foundational facts, provided by NSS and subject to revision, improvement, and evolution over time themselves as methods and data improve.

Inspired by this list of possible exposure models
Social science in the positivist tradition has been focused on determining whether X affects Y and more precisely whether X is a causal factor in Y.
Social science has been plagued by the fact that most social systems are not simple linear systems with factors that can be isolated and repeated. The causal project has tried many ways to get around this, but this is just tweaking causality at the margins. Many useful contributions have been made, but the limitations are much greater. A more appropriate model of the social world is that of atmospheric modeling - a complex, highly interactive system with dramatic feedback loops operating at multiple scales, simultaneously. Meteorologists have focused on ever improving prediction and description instead of identifying causal factors via natural experiments.
The social world is not similar to repeated agricultural experiments that the positivist tradition of experimentation has its roots in. Indeed, agriculture (biology) has moved away from this model itself with the advent of biomarkers, genetic modeling, and complex weather and soil science.
This is the direction social science needs to move. In the era of big data there have been remarkable advances in social science, but many of these have also come with concerns about ethics, privacy, and transparency. Inside Facebook complex big data methods are used to identify and promote content that is most likely to keep us using Facebook.
Public social science has not kept up.
### **What is needed is a new social science.**
Instead of focusing on causal inference, we need to reorient social science towards prediction, description, and forecasting. We need to think about measuring and describing social risk, risk mitigation, and recovery from risk. This not only makes social science pragmatic again, it also turns social science away from asking questions everyone knows the answer to (e.g. do teachers matter for quality education) and reorients it toward providing information that people can act on in the social world.
We now sit at the intersection of data and technologies that can allow us to model the social world in a similar way meteorologists model the physical world, with a focus on giving practical and actionable, but appropriately bounded, predictions.
The advent of these technologies is needed:
*   Geospatial data
    
*   Massive parallel computing power
    
*   Freely available geo-referenced datasets
    
*   Core ground truth data across many domains
    
Here I propose an outline for how this new social science should function, and then provide specific examples.
### **New Social Science**
In general our methodology will be built as follows:
*   An **allocation model** is used to take aggregate observations and plausibly array them in latitude/longitude coordinates, or to connect study observations directly to spatially referenced data at appropriate resolutions
    
*   **Exposure models** are used to identify the exposure of each point to various risk factors through spatial referencing, temporal interpolation, and smoothing/process modeling to generate our best estimates of exposure to various factors
    
*   An **outcome model** is applied to forecast an outcome based on the exposure models. Importantly, the outcome model need not be predicting some "social outcome" but could instead be aggregating and communicating the combined risk of the exposure models.
    
Underlying all of these models are data sources and each component of the models can adjusted as experts learn more about how to model these discrete aspects. This is similar to the interactions among many complex models that underly the modern marvel of weather forecasting (and is key to how weather forecasting improved.)
**Let's imagine we want to estimate the impacts of incarceration and arrest on educational attainment.** The positivist way to do this may be to survey or gather administrative records in one jurisdiction and follow students for a long period of time, conduct matching or look for a natural experiment, and measure using causal inference.
As an alternative, let's look the New Social Science approach which operates at scale, can be refined and improved over time, combines and accumulates knowledge across social science disciplines, and maintains and respects the privacy of the individuals in the study while centering their experience.
### **Allocation Model**
We have student enrollment data for a school district in a large metropolitan area by race. We infer almost all students at the district live within the physical boundaries of the school district. We also have the school district boundaries and from the US Census we know the population of a grid within the school district, by Census block by race and age. We can combine this with data about roads to infer where students are most likely to live based on their race/age. We can difference out uninhabitable areas using roadway and greenspace data.
The allocation model is better if we have addresses and outcomes for our study participants, but we can use, for some questions, a form of inference to infer the location and permutate over possible locations for individual records when tihs is not available. **This is an area that needs active study.**
### **Exposure Model**
We have four types of exposure data:
*   Arrests by age and race for all police jurisdictions in the school district boundary
    
*   Latitude and longitude of police killings of civilians within the boundary
    
*   Race/Sex/School counts of arrests of students at school within the district
    
*   Police deployment data inferred from police "calls for service" data from a public portal
    
In addition, we may want to construct a general deprivation exposure model that describes resource disparities individuals within the school district may experience. We could use, for example, HUD data on economic and housing deprivation. We could also from the points infer household income.
This strategy in the public health world is known as **geomarker assessment** - using geospatial/temporal information to infer risk factor exposure.

If the students we allocated had an outcome attached to them, we could then predict it.

### **Benefits of this approach:**
Tied to ecological inference
*   Scale
    
*   Reclaims the value of description
    
*   Scales to different researchers
    
*   Protects privacy
    
*   Better accumulates knowledge (integrates expertise across domains as model components)
    
*   Remains verifiable
    
More importantly, this is social science that is rooted in the complexity of its subjects lives. The exposure models can be thought of as more and more sophisticated ways of trying to understand the lived experience of people, at scale. The allocation model is a more precise way of distinguishing the lives people live by their social and physical location. Instead of our data being observations, through greater and greater combinations of allocation and exposure models, we get closer toward a social science that can describe the life trajectories of individual people in plausible and recognizable ways.
This also unifies social scientists.
Computation and scale  
The above example requires some local data, but also relies predominantly on a broad national data architecture. Where local data is required, exposure models could be created that can infer it when it is not available - a more sophisticated form of imputation. This is much like how atmospheric data is modeled with gaps in the global model being filled in by different models.
Also like atmospheric models this approach is coherent across resolutions. We can zoom out and do our analysis at the county level or zoom in and try to allocate individuals to residential addresses, but the same methods and modeling procedures will apply. The level of resolution will depend on data availability and in most cases that depends on the availability of the outcome data. In cases where we are describing and not predicting (unsupervised) we will find constraints in our resolution based on the variability in the underlying exposure models across space/time.
We can also generate uncertainty intervals with respect to any of the input models by randomizing them and restarting. So we can imagine thousands of possible allocations of people based on our allocation model and we can generate our output over thousands of iterations of the output model and assess reliability/stability. This is how hurricane forecasts are done to great success.
Examples:
*   Modeling risk and trauma exposure using residential segregation police deployment estimation, and violence
    
*   Modeling chronic illness exposure using air and water toxic data, residential housing, and resource deprivation/healthcare access
    
*   Modeling white nationalism using police and education inequality, segregation, and resource deprivation
