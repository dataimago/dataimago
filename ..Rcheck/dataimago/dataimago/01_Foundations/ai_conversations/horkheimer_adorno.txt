## The Frankfurt School and the Project of Critical Theory: Horkheimer and Adorno

### I. Introduction to the Frankfurt School

The Frankfurt School refers to a group of interdisciplinary scholars associated with the Institute for Social Research (Institut für Sozialforschung) founded in 1923 at Goethe University Frankfurt. The thinkers most commonly associated with this intellectual tradition—Max Horkheimer, Theodor Adorno, Herbert Marcuse, Erich Fromm, and later Jürgen Habermas—sought to examine society, culture, and economy through a fusion of Marxist analysis, psychoanalysis, sociology, and philosophy. They were united in their belief that Enlightenment ideals had failed to deliver human emancipation and had instead become complicit in systems of domination, most catastrophically illustrated by the rise of fascism and the Holocaust.

### II. Critical Theory vs. Traditional Theory

Max Horkheimer’s 1937 essay *“Traditional and Critical Theory”* serves as a manifesto for the Frankfurt School’s intellectual agenda. He distinguished between two types of theory:

* **Traditional theory** is aligned with the natural sciences and is characterized by objectivity, neutrality, and a focus on explaining the world. It assumes a subject-object dichotomy and aims to produce knowledge that can be applied to technical or instrumental problems. In this respect, traditional theory is closely associated with positivism—particularly the idea that knowledge is value-free and grounded in empirical verification.

* **Critical theory**, in contrast, is self-reflective, historical, and normatively oriented. It does not simply seek to explain or predict phenomena but aims to *critique* social structures with the goal of human emancipation. Critical theory thus insists that knowledge is always situated within social, historical, and political contexts, and that theory should be oriented toward the transformation of society rather than its passive description.

Crucially, critical theory is both epistemological and ethical—it interrogates the conditions under which knowledge is produced and the purposes that knowledge serves. It resists the positivist tendency to divorce facts from values and instead insists that social inquiry must be directed toward the realization of human freedom, justice, and dignity.

### III. The Dialectic of Enlightenment: Rationality Turned Against Itself

One of the central works of Horkheimer and Adorno’s collaboration is *Dialectic of Enlightenment* (1944), a searing critique of Enlightenment rationality. Their argument is paradoxical and deeply unsettling: the Enlightenment’s project of liberating humanity through reason has paradoxically culminated in new forms of domination. Rather than achieving autonomy, rationality has become instrumental and administrative—a “means without ends.” Bureaucratic rationality, capitalist efficiency, and positivist science have come to dominate not just institutions but the structure of consciousness itself.

This critique is developed through historical and philosophical analysis as well as cultural criticism. For example, in their analysis of the “culture industry,” Horkheimer and Adorno argue that mass-produced entertainment pacifies the population, suppresses critical thought, and perpetuates conformity. Culture becomes commodified, and individuals are reduced to passive consumers. This too is an outcome of instrumental reason—the same rationality that builds efficient machines also standardizes human experience.

### IV. Fusion of Hermeneutics and Positivism: Toward Ethical Critique

A distinctive feature of critical theory is its aspiration to *reconcile* two intellectual traditions often seen as opposed: **hermeneutics** (interpretive understanding of meaning, rooted in humanistic disciplines) and **positivism** (empirical observation and causal explanation, rooted in natural sciences).

Horkheimer and Adorno believed that social science must:

1. **Interpret the meanings and subjective experiences** of individuals within a society (a hermeneutic task);
2. **Understand the causal structures and systemic forces** that shape those experiences (a positivistic task);
3. **Critique both** in light of the ethical imperative to reduce suffering and increase human flourishing.

In doing so, critical theory positions itself against both the cold empiricism of positivism and the abstract idealism of traditional philosophy. It insists that knowledge must be grounded in a material analysis of society—class structures, economic relations, historical development—but must also take seriously the interior, lived experiences of human beings, their hopes, fears, and alienation.

This approach is exemplified in *Dialectic of Enlightenment*, where myth, literature, psychology, and political economy are woven into a tapestry of critique. Rather than abandoning the rigor of empirical inquiry, Horkheimer and Adorno sought to place it in service of a broader ethical and emancipatory vision.

### V. Ethics Without Foundations

One challenge that Horkheimer and Adorno confronted was the question of ethical grounding. In rejecting both religious absolutes and Enlightenment rationalism as foundations for morality, they faced the problem of how to justify normative claims. Their solution was not to propose new universal principles, but to adopt a *negative dialectics*: critique that is aware of its own historical limits, that refuses premature closure, and that maintains a commitment to the abolition of suffering as its minimal ethical foundation.

Adorno, especially, emphasized the importance of keeping open the possibility of human suffering as a moral fact that cannot be reduced to theory. In *Negative Dialectics* and *Minima Moralia*, he insists that philosophy must remain critical, tentative, and attentive to what resists totalization.

### VI. Legacy and Influence

The fusion of hermeneutic and positivist methods in the service of ethical critique remains one of the Frankfurt School’s most enduring contributions. Their work laid the foundation for later developments in critical theory, including Habermas’s theory of communicative action and contemporary critiques of neoliberalism, technology, and ideology.

Their project is deeply relevant today: in a world where data science often claims neutrality, and where technical solutions abound without sufficient reflection on ends, Horkheimer and Adorno’s reminder is crucial—that reason without ethical purpose can reinforce domination rather than alleviate it.
